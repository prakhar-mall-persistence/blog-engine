What is Vapi?

Vapi is a developer platform for building voice AI agents — software that answers or places phone calls and holds a spoken conversation. You define what the agent should do, connect it to a phone number, and it handles the call.

The broader category is "voice agents." These are systems that can take an inbound support call, qualify an outbound lead, book an appointment, or route a caller to the right person. Vapi is one of several platforms in this space, alongside tools like Bland AI, Retell AI, and Persistence.

If you are researching Vapi, you are usually deciding one of two things: whether to build a voice agent at all, or which platform to build it on. This post covers how these systems work under the hood so you can judge them on the same terms.

How voice AI works

A voice agent is a pipeline. Audio comes in, gets transcribed, gets reasoned over, gets turned back into speech, and goes out. All of this happens while the caller is on the line, so it has to be fast.

There are three core models in the loop. Speech-to-text (STT) converts the caller's words into text. A large language model (LLM) reads that text, decides what to do, and writes a reply. Text-to-speech (TTS) turns the reply back into audio. Around those models sits the call infrastructure: telephony, turn-taking, interruption handling, and any integrations the agent needs to look something up or take an action.

The hard part is not any single model. It is the seams between them. Every handoff adds delay, and delay is what makes an agent feel robotic. A good platform hides this complexity and keeps the round trip short.

Why latency is the metric that matters

In a real conversation, people expect a reply within a fraction of a second. When a voice agent takes too long to respond, the caller talks over it, repeats themselves, or hangs up. Latency is the single most important quality signal in voice AI.

The delay adds up across the pipeline: time to detect the caller stopped talking, time to transcribe, time for the model to think, time to synthesize speech, and network round trips on top. Shaving milliseconds at each stage is most of the engineering work.

This is why voice platforms talk about response time so much. Persistence targets sub-100ms responses through its architecture, which is aggressive by design — the goal is a conversation that does not have gaps a human would notice.

What to look for in a voice AI platform

Voice quality is the easiest thing to demo and the least reliable thing to judge on. Two systems can sound identical in a scripted demo and behave completely differently on live calls. Look deeper.

Check four things. First, latency on real calls, not marketing numbers — test it yourself. Second, integrations: can the agent transfer to a human with context, check a calendar, call your API, or update a record? Third, tooling: can you build, test, and monitor agents in one place, or are you stitching tools together? Fourth, pricing structure: pay-as-you-go with no seat minimum costs roughly what your calls cost, while seat and annual commitments change the math on a pilot.

Rates on every platform change, so check the current vendor pricing pages on the day you compare rather than trusting a number quoted in an article.
